The art and science of prompting · 1

Tell Copilot what you need

Prompts are how you ask Copilot to do something for you — creating, summarizing, editing, or transforming. Think about prompting like having a conversation: plain but clear language, and the context you would give an assistant.

Learn about projects and concepts

“What is Project X and who are the key stakeholders working on it?”

Edit text

“Check this product launch rationale for inconsistencies.”

Transform documents

“Transform this FAQ doc into a 10-slide onboarding guide.”

Summarize information

“Write a session abstract of this presentation.”

Create engaging content

“Create a value proposition for Product X.”

Catch-up on missed items

“Provide a summary of the updates and action items on Project X.”

Your first prompt

All four ingredients in one real request. Copy it, paste it into Copilot, and watch how it reasons through a multi-part question — then checks its own answer against sources.

Before you run this

Set the model to GPT 5.6 — Think Deeper in the model picker at the top of the Copilot chat. This question has three parts and asks for verification, so it needs the reasoning model rather than the fast default.

The prompt
Explain how quantum computation will impact financial industry. Explain the following: 1. What is quantum computing and why is it being researched and devolped - to general or non-engineer audience. 2. What are the finance use cases that can be adressed better by quantum computing? 3. How confident is general industry to quantum currently? Verify the explanation against trusted sources.
Why it worksA clear multi-part goal, an audience in the context, and a source instruction that forces Copilot to ground and cite rather than assume.

The art and science of prompting · 3

Keep the conversation going

Following up on your prompts helps you collaborate with Copilot, to gain more useful, tailored responses. You are never stuck with the first answer.

Generating content ideas

Lead with broader requests, then give specific details about the content.

Enabling insightful meetings

Request a meeting recap, then ask for more information about what you should know.

Storytelling assistance

Ask Copilot to write a story, then guide it by giving more specific, relevant details.

Gaining insights

Ask for a summary of a specific file, then ask relevant questions to gain deeper insights.

Translating languages

Ask Copilot to translate a sentence into a supported language, then ask for more context or a regional dialect.

Solving technical problems

Present a technical problem, then narrow it down, or ask for step-by-step guidance.

    Best practice 1

    Include details

    Try both prompts in Copilot and compare the responses. Which one gave you a richer, more usable briefing note?

    Prompt 1 · thin
    Write a briefing note about digital economy cooperation.
    Prompt 2 · all four ingredients
    Craft a 1,000-word briefing note for senior officials attending an interregional including regions / countries dialogue on digital cooperation, explaining the significance and benefits of cross-border digital trade facilitation, such as mutual recognition of digital identity and paperless customs. Draw on published intergovernmental sources and real regional examples. Include the main implementation challenges, and conclude with three recommended next steps for a national digital agency.
    TipThe second prompt works because it names the audience, the sources, and the shape of the answer.
    Best practice 2

    Provide specific instructions

    Copilot is built to take action, so telling it what to do — or what not to do — can be very effective.

    Prompt 1
    Write an overview of how AI can improve efficiency in public administration. Generate 3 bullet points.
    Prompt 2 · with a negative instruction
    Write a 3-day agenda for a national capacity-building workshop for country / region on digital government for civil servants from two ministries with mixed technical backgrounds. Don’t include any vendor product names or pricing.
    TipA negative instruction — what NOT to include — is often the fastest way to narrow an answer.
    Best practice 3

    Provide a style or format

    Copilot can use style information if you give it in the prompt. This makes the output easier to use — or saves you a formatting step entirely. Append any of these to a prompt:

    …format it as a table with one row per clause
    …give it as a bullet list a minister can read in two minutes
    …make every statutory or treaty reference bold
    …write it in the style of a formal note verbale
    …keep it to one page, plain language, and expand every acronym on first use
    …give me three versions: one line, one paragraph, one page
    …use the same section headings as the document I attached
    …write it so it still reads clearly after translation
    TipName the format you will actually paste into — a table, a bullet list, a one-page note.
    Best practice 4

    Give examples

    If you provide examples in your prompt, Copilot uses them as a model for its output. One example of the tone you want does more work than three sentences describing it.

    Prompt 1
    Write a one-sentence public summary of a new cross-border data protection rule for citizens with no legal background, using the following line as inspiration for tone: “Your data stays yours — we just make sure it travels safely.”
    Prompt 2
    Draft three closing lines for a Minister of X’s speech on regional of country / region digital cooperation, using these two lines as inspiration for tone: “We are not digitising government. We are rebuilding trust.” “The test of a digital service is the citizen who almost gave up.”
    TipOne example of the tone you want does more than three sentences describing it.
    Best practice 5

    Give feedback

    Some prompts won’t give you what you expected first time. Tell Copilot what was wrong and ask again in the same chat — you are refining, not starting over.

    Round 1Too general → ask for specifics
    →
    Round 2Add the decision being asked for
    →
    Round 3Cut the length, drop the jargon
    Prompt 1
    Your answer is too general for a policy audience — add specific implementation steps and name the responsible institution for each one.
    Prompt 2
    Add a closing sentence stating the single decision we are asking the Minister to take.
    Prompt 3
    That is closer. Keep the structure, but cut it to half the length and drop the jargon.
    TipYou are not starting over — keep correcting in the same chat until the answer is usable.
    Best practice 6

    Mind your grammar!

    AI is sensitive to grammar and spelling. Punctuation in particular can change the meaning of your request — and the scope of the answer you get back.

    Without commas
    Draft talking points on supporting exporters small businesses and farmers
    With commas
    Draft talking points on supporting exporters, small businesses, and farmers

    Without the commas, the first can be read as one category — “exporters’ small businesses” — and Copilot narrows the output accordingly. Same words, different policy scope.

    TipRead your prompt aloud before you send it. If it is ambiguous to you, it is ambiguous to Copilot.
    Prompt examples

    Two worked examples

    Four ingredients, then the finished prompt. Highlighted chips show where you attach your own document — we supply no sample files.

    Example 1 — harmonising a draft against a regional commitment

    GoalCompare a draft national regulation against a regional commitment
    ContextI’m a director in a ministry preparing an inter-agency meeting
    SourceA document you attach yourself — your draft, and the instrument to align it to
    ExpectationsAligned, divergent and missing clauses, with every article cited
    The promptAttach a file
    I’m a your role preparing an inter-agency mention agencies / parties meeting. Using the two documents I have attached, compare them clause by clause and show me where they align, where they diverge, and where the draft is silent. Present it as a table with the cited article for every row, and flag anything a legal officer must verify.

    Example 2 — picking up a project or portfolio you’ve just been moved into

    GoalGet across a file fast enough to speak to it
    ContextI was rotated into this portfolio and I have a meeting tomorrow
    SourceA document you attach yourself — the main policy paper on your new file
    ExpectationsPlain language, no jargon, with the practical effect spelled out
    The promptAttach a file
    I was rotated into this project / portfolio last week and I have an inter-agency mention agencies / parties meeting tomorrow. Based on the document I have attached, bring me up to speed on what this policy does and where it stands. Use plain language, expand every acronym on first use, and end with the three questions I should be ready to answer in the room.

    1 · Nothing attached — Copilot Chat, then Word or PowerPoint
    Research how governments of countries / regions are using AI in public service delivery, then draft a 6-slide deck introducing the topic to senior officials. Assume the audience is non-technical and has not used AI tools before. Use publicly available sources from the last two years and cite them. One idea per slide, a plain-language title on each, and end with three questions a government should ask before adopting AI.
    2 · Your own documentAttach a file
    Using the document I have attached, summarise it for someone who has never seen it, then pull out the decisions it asks for and anything left unresolved. Give me a short summary, decisions with who must take each, and open questions. Say clearly if something I would expect to find is missing.
    3 · Your whole mailbox — Copilot in Outlook
    I have been away and I am behind on my correspondence. Look across my mailbox from the last two weeks and tell me what needs my attention. Group it into: awaiting my reply, awaiting my decision, and for information only. For each item give the sender, date, one line on the ask, and flag passed deadlines.
